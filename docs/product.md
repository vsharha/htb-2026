# Product

## What it is

The audience heatmap shows a startup where in a city its target customers live, work and pass through, and where it can find evidence of competitors' outdoor advertising. A startup fills in a short form about its product, customer, budget and markets. It gets back a map of the city with audience fit, competition evidence, and placement recommendations. Recommendations use observed gaps when current campaigns have precise locations; otherwise they use audience fit and state that competition coverage is unknown. The run ends with specific placements to buy, such as LinkNYC kiosks and bus shelters, each with an estimated cost per audience member and a way to measure results. The demo city is New York.

It is for startups that want customers beyond Meta and Google but have no media agency and a limited budget.

Three things set it apart from audience-mapping products and outdoor ad marketplaces:

- It maps one startup's target customer, described in a sentence, rather than a fixed set of audience segments.
- It gathers evidence of where competitors have advertised, using an agent that searches the web and reads campaign pages. Areas without verified campaigns have unknown competition coverage.
- It ends with placements a startup can buy in those areas, not just a map.

## Decisions

Every decision the user makes or confirms, including ones reported from teammates: who the product serves, data sources, methods or models, the stack, how parts connect, and the demo scope. Track and challenge priorities are recorded in `AGENTS.md`, not here. One line each: the decision, the reason, and the date. A changed decision is updated in place, so this section always describes the current plan.

- Product idea: the audience heatmap, which maps where a startup's target customers live, work and pass through in a city and where competitors leave them unreached; details in `ideas/audience-heatmap.md`. It replaces the ad channel mispricing finder (`ideas/ad-channel-mispricing.md`), keeping its onboarding form, because public data about people and places is far richer than published prices for advertising placements. 2026-11-07.
- Web app: Next.js 16 (App Router, TypeScript, Tailwind CSS 4, ESLint) managed with pnpm, in the `web/` folder rather than the repo root. The repo root stays for docs and agent config, and a second service such as a Python API can sit beside `web/` later. 2026-11-07.
- Frontend UI: shadcn/ui components styled with Tailwind CSS v4. 2026-11-07.
- LLM calls and AI agents: Vercel AI SDK in the web app. Jev uses Vercel AI Gateway. The competitor-campaign agent uses OpenAI directly through `@ai-sdk/openai` and `OPENAI_API_KEY`, as chosen by the user on 2026-11-07. The Python scripts in `tools/` call no language model. Python prepares the numerical dataset, and everything that depends on one startup's form runs in the web app. 2026-11-07.
- AI model configuration: model defaults live in `AI_MODELS` in `web/src/lib/server/ai.ts`, so switching models requires one code edit and no model environment variables. The defaults are `openai:gpt-5.6-luna` for competitor research and `gateway:typesafe-ai/jev` for audience decisions. The user selected GPT-5.6 Luna for research on 2026-11-07. The Vercel AI SDK provider registry resolves both. The research adapter keeps its model, hosted search tools, provider options, credential checks, source decoding and error messages together. Audience scoring resolves a decision model separately. Environment variables supply credentials only. 2026-11-07.
- Audience fit scoring: Jev, TypeSafe AI's decision model, scores the target-customer sentence against each column of the prebuilt tract dataset, and the web app weights the columns by those scores. Jev answers declared yes/no, choice and score questions with probabilities instead of writing text, and is called as `typesafe-ai/jev` through AI Gateway. The tract data is numbers, which code combines without a model, so Jev's part is turning the sentence into weights. Weights are signed, because the direction depends on the customer: a high income is good for an affluent audience and bad for a low-income one, and `median_age` points the opposite way for young and older audiences. The weighted score divides by the sum of the absolute weights, because signed weights can sum to zero and make a plain average unstable. One request costs about $0.00001. 2026-11-07.
- The onboarding form asks for industry, a one-sentence description, a one-sentence target customer, what a customer pays, monthly advertising budget, and markets. It does not ask B2B or B2C, which the target customer covers. The form reads as one sentence, "Our company is … in …. We want to reach … who would pay $… per …", which fits shops, services and B2B companies as well as startups, and describes customers the company wants rather than ones it has (2026-11-08). Budget filters out placements with minimum spends, and what a customer pays stands in for lifetime value in the audience weighting because it is easier to answer. 2026-11-07.
- Submitting the completely empty onboarding form runs the sample fintech brief for a budgeting app for shared flats, targeting young professionals in tech who rent with roommates, at $12 per month and a $3,000 monthly advertising budget in New York. Entering any field or changing a dropdown requires the complete form. The sample uses the main form flow so it needs no separate `/map` route. 2026-11-07.
- Submitting the onboarding form moves the whole results view upward from below the screen over 1.2 seconds, so the transition shows the new view arriving over the form. The background gradient rotates steadily at 150 degrees per second behind it until the results cover the form. Reduced-motion preferences skip the visible movement. 2026-11-08.
- Product name: Placewise, shown in the web app's header. The form's markets field is a city menu whose only city is New York, with a disabled "more cities coming" entry, so the demo presents New York as the first of several cities. The form maps all five boroughs without listing them. 2026-11-08.
- Demo city: New York, because it publishes the most detailed public data on residents, workers, trips and advertising locations. The method is meant to work in any city with similar data. 2026-11-07.
- Map markers open no popups. Clicking a placement or campaign marker highlights and scrolls to its row in the results column, switching to the Competitors tab for a campaign, as decided by the user on 2026-11-08. Popups drew beneath placement markers and repeated what the column already shows.
- The output is one map with audience fit, competition evidence, and recommendations. It has two tabs, Customers and Competitors, until a current campaign has a station or address match. A third tab, Gap, then appears and ranks high audience fit with low observed competition. Gap opens by default once it exists, unless the user has already picked another tab. Without a Gap tab, the Customers tab lists the placements, chosen by audience fit and labelled as not confirmed gaps. Without qualifying evidence, the Competitors view shows campaign markers without colouring tracts as low competition. Missing evidence remains unknown coverage. This keeps useful recommendations without presenting audience fit as a verified advertising gap, and without a third tab that would repeat the Customers ranking. Decided by the user on 2026-11-08.
- Map: MapLibre GL through react-map-gl, on CARTO's light Positron basemap. MapLibre recolours every tract from one paint setting when the layer changes, and react-map-gl is maintained by the team behind deck.gl; CARTO's basemap needs no API key and is run by a company, where OpenFreeMap, the other keyless option, has one maintainer. The browser loads the tract shapes from a Route Handler that serves `data/analysis/nyc_tracts.geojson`, so the data keeps one copy in `data/`. 2026-11-07.
- Competitor research uses the Vercel AI SDK's `ToolLoopAgent`, with OpenAI web search and page reading, as chosen by the user on 2026-11-07. Competitors are the companies already advertising outdoors in New York to the same customer: discovery searches from the startup's product and target customer, not its industry label or named direct rivals, and keeps companies that sell a substitute or target the same customer, as decided by the user on 2026-11-08. Searching named direct rivals rarely finds campaigns, because small rivals seldom buy outdoor advertising, and the industry label alone returns unrelated mass-market brands. The code separates competitor discovery from each competitor's placement search, so repeated searches for one brand cannot consume another brand’s budget. Discovery has ten model steps and 60 seconds and reports three to six competitors. It also records every campaign its searches turn up, including ones that name no place, and prefers recent campaigns, as decided by the user on 2026-11-08, because earlier searches saw far more sources than they reported. Each then gets eight model steps and 65 seconds to search for stations, street addresses and dates, running concurrently within a 165-second total deadline. Each competitor can contribute six rows with a map location and three without, counted separately so that vague findings stay as a fallback without crowding out specific ones, as decided by the user on 2026-11-08. One finding can name several places, each becoming its own row. The model reports competitors and campaigns through typed tools. No public campaign list has been identified. Source links must appear in that run's research results. Findings keep their evidence, locations and known dates. All reliably located findings have map markers, including historical campaigns and unknown dates. Only current campaigns with a matched station or street address affect the heatmap; approximate areas do not. Research outcomes distinguish completion, partial results, failure and timeout, with search counts, source URLs found, rejected source submissions and competitor completion counts. Findings without mapped locations or confirmed current dates remain visible but do not establish current coverage. Missing evidence means unknown coverage. Stores and offices remain planned as separate evidence of competitor presence, not proof of advertising. 2026-11-08.
- Campaign geocoding: Geoapify, using `GEOAPIFY_API_KEY` on the server, as confirmed by the user on 2026-11-07. This places billboards and other campaigns without forcing a subway-station match. Street corners, such as "41st Street and 7th Avenue, Manhattan", are never sent to Geoapify, because its free-text search returns a different corner with full confidence; they are placed from a table of New York street intersections built from NYC Open Data's street centerline, decided on 2026-11-08. A corner that exists in more than one borough is placed only when the source names the borough or a neighbourhood in it. Lookups are restricted to New York; broad landmarks such as Times Square and neighbourhoods are labelled approximate. A campaign whose source names a place gets a marker there. A campaign that names only the city, a borough or the transit network gets no marker and is labelled as having no fixed location, as decided by the user on 2026-11-08, because any point on the map would claim a presence no source supports. Successful lookups are cached for 24 hours in each server process, up to 500 entries. The map does not credit Geoapify, as decided by the user on 2026-11-08; Geoapify's terms require that credit only on its free plan. Each mapped campaign's row in the Competitors tab retains the data-source attribution, such as OpenStreetMap, which the terms require on every plan. Dates and location precision are separate: an undated or approximate campaign can be shown without being counted as current competition. 2026-11-07.
- Each run ends with up to 12 specific placements to buy, such as LinkNYC kiosks and bus shelters, each with an estimated cost per audience member. Recommendations follow descending observed gap scores when current campaigns have precise locations, or descending audience fit otherwise, with at most one placement per tract and at least 500 metres between placements. The search continues through lower-ranked tracts when candidates are missing or too close, so one cluster cannot fill the list. A map alone is something audience-mapping products already offer, and a placement is something a startup can act on. 2026-11-08.
- Superseded on 8 November 2026 by the user: placements no longer carry a way to measure them (a QR code, promo code or landing page), because the options were invented and gave no information. Originally: every recommendation came with one, because marketers avoid channels they can't measure. 2026-11-07.
- Superseded on 2026-11-07 by the pivot to the audience heatmap, which leaves out airports: Demo scope: confirm the Revolut airport mispricing with real data, by checking the source's figures (cost per thousand impressions and per thousand attentive seconds against Meta, and the breakeven attention) against published prices, audience counts and attention studies. The source's model rests on assumptions, so the aim is to test them. 2026-11-07.
- The web app runs each startup's result live when the form is submitted, so a startup typed in at the demo gets its own map. Only the city dataset is prepared ahead of time. Jev and the agent run in Node.js inside the web app, so no Python runs behind it. 2026-11-07.
- How the web app's server does the work: a Server Function takes the form, reads the tract data and column list from `data/`, calls Jev, and returns an audience fit score per tract ID. The browser loads the tract shapes once, not on every submission. The competitor-campaign agent runs in a Route Handler that streams typed events with `createUIMessageStream` and `createUIMessageStreamResponse`. The browser uses `DefaultChatTransport` and `readUIMessageStream` to decode them. A Server Function returns only when it finishes, and Next.js runs a client's Server Functions one at a time. The server matches explicitly named subway stations and uses Geoapify to locate source-reported landmarks and street addresses. Ambiguous, citywide, weak or outside-city matches remain unmapped with an explanation. The data stays in `data/`, and `outputFileTracingRoot` and `outputFileTracingIncludes` in `next.config.ts` include the analysis, station table and recorded research in the deployment. 2026-11-07.
- What the Python analysis writes for the web app, all in `data/`: the street intersections used to place campaigns at street corners, the tract shapes, and a tract table with each feature column also scaled from 0 to 1 so weighted columns can be added; a column list giving each column's name, a plain description and whether it measures living, working or passing through, from which the web app builds its Jev questions; the placements with their tract; and the subway stations. 2026-11-07.
- The Python scripts are one library module per data source, each with a `load()` that returns its data and downloads it first only if nothing is cached, plus one analysis entry point that imports them, joins them per census tract and writes the city dataset the web app reads. Caching makes reruns fast and lets the demo work offline. 2026-11-07.
- Build order: the frontend is built first against stand-in server calls, and the real Server Function and Route Handler come once the frontend is mostly done. The stand-ins return the same types the backend will, from `web/src/lib/contract.ts`, so the UI does not change when they are swapped. 2026-11-07.
- New York data is gathered ahead of time with scripts in `tools/`. A recorded live run of the competitor-campaign agent is kept as a fallback for the sample brief. It is used only when live research fails before returning findings and the city, industry, product and target customer match the recording. Every results tab labels recorded research with its capture date. Other briefs never inherit the sample's competitors. The source module `tools.sources.competitor_run` captures a complete run from the running web app without calling a model directly. 2026-11-07.
- Competitor logos: each competitor's own website is recorded alongside its name, and the app shows the site's icon from DuckDuckGo's favicon cache, in the competitors list, on each of that competitor's campaign rows and on its campaign markers, because a name alone does not make a competitor on the map or in the ads list recognisable at a glance. A competitor without a usable site, or whose icon fails to load, shows its initials. The research agent reports the website, so no logo files or manual curation are needed. 2026-11-08.
- Pitch deck: a `/presentation` route inside the web app, built with Spectacle and themed with the app's existing CSS variables and fonts, with no new colours, so it matches the app in light and dark mode. Five slides, one idea each with speaker notes: cover, problem, how it works, live demo and next steps. The demo slide embeds the running app in an iframe, the deck loads in the browser only, and it exports to PDF through printing. 2026-11-08.
- Colour-blind palette: the settings menu offers a fifth palette, navy and amber, whose heat ramp darkens from light to dark instead of changing hue, so a reader who cannot tell the hues apart still ranks the tracts. The user asked for it on 2026-11-08, after analysis found that the existing four put their lightest heat colour at the middle of the ramp, which leaves the score dots in the rankings non-monotonic. That analysis also found the default palette's amber-to-red pair loses its hue distinction under protanopia and deuteranopia, where it survives only by lightness. The other four palettes stay as they are, and the settings menu labels this one "Colour-blind friendly" under its name. 2026-11-08.
